After the Campaign: How Hexi Became a Han Frontier

战事之后：河西走廊如何成为汉朝边郡

三角城的土墙见证了早期聚落；数百年后，河西的道路、农田和居民将面对另一种划定边界的力量。

English Story

01In 121 BCE, control of the Hexi Corridor changed through war and surrender. That sentence sounds simple on a map, but it raises a harder question: how does an army's victory become a lasting frontier? The previous article examined Sanjiao, where people had built an earth enclosure centuries earlier. Its wall cannot be linked directly to the Han state or to a single later community. What it does show is that the corridor already held places worth organizing and protecting. By the second century BCE, the Xiongnu confederation held much of Hexi, including its western and eastern sectors under different leaders. Han expansion entered a lived landscape, not an empty road waiting to be opened.

02Emperor Wu's government had been fighting the Xiongnu along several fronts. Hexi mattered because it lay between the Han heartland and routes farther west, while its mountains, rivers, and grazing land also supported people already there. In 121 BCE, the Han general Huo Qubing led campaigns against Xiongnu forces in the region. Later that year, the Xiongnu leader Hunye and his followers submitted to the Han. Accounts of the campaign come chiefly from Han historical writing, which tells us much about court strategy but less about the choices of ordinary residents. The records do not establish that every family departed or that all local groups had the same interests.

03The change placed difficult demands on the Han court. A distant territory could not be held by a victory report alone. Troops needed food, water, shelter, and a way to send information back across hundreds of kilometers. An oasis could support a settlement, but the dry ground between oases made supply uncertain. The government therefore began turning military control into administrative control. A commandery was a regional unit with officials and subordinate counties; it could register households, organize labor, and support garrisons. This was a new layer of authority over a corridor where earlier settlements and pastoral networks had followed different patterns.

04Later sources name four Hexi commanderies, from east to west: Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan, and Dunhuang. They did not appear as a finished system on the day of Huo's campaign. The Book of Han describes Jiuquan first, followed by the movement of settlers and the division of additional commanderies. Modern scholars disagree about the exact founding dates of some of the four, especially Dunhuang, because passages in the ancient records differ. What is secure is the sequence: military acquisition came first; institutions, settlement, and fortified routes developed afterward. The names on a map record a long process of governing people and places, not four identical acts of naming.

05Settlement made this system possible and costly. Han authorities moved people from the interior toward the new frontier, while soldiers were stationed to guard and cultivate land. For those sent west, an imperial decision meant work and residence in an unfamiliar, dry region. Farming near an oasis required dependable water and repeated labor, not merely a line drawn around a field. For existing communities, new officials and military farms would have changed how claims to land, labor, and movement were decided. The sources do not let us reconstruct the response of each local household, and they do not justify treating Xiongnu followers, earlier farmers, and new settlers as one population.

06Defense also became a daily task rather than a single battle. Han authorities built walls, forts, watchtowers, and passes along the corridor. At its western end, Yumenguan and Yangguan came to regulate passage beyond Dunhuang. A traveler or envoy now encountered checkpoints; a soldier might have to watch a beacon or keep a written record. Archaeological finds and excavated documents from the Han frontier show that this network depended on routine administration as well as weapons. It made communication and guarded travel more reliable for the state, while also bringing routes and residents under closer supervision. The road was becoming an institution, not simply a path through open land.

07The new frontier was not created for trade alone. Han rulers sought security against Xiongnu power and access toward the Western Regions; the garrisons and administrative centers served those aims. Yet the same facilities could later support envoys, merchants, animals, and goods moving through the oases. This is why a familiar label such as 'Silk Road' can mislead when placed too early in the story. People and crops had crossed parts of Hexi long before 121 BCE, as the earlier articles showed. What changed was the scale of state investment and the attempt to channel movement through an enduring system of offices, farms, walls, and passes.

08Seen from Sanjiao, the contrast is sharp but not a direct family history. Its ancient wall marked one local settlement; the Han frontier linked many places under distant authority. Some former Xiongnu subjects entered a new political order, newcomers arrived from the Han interior, and older communities would have had to navigate changing rules around land and movement. The surviving evidence is uneven: court histories emphasize rulers and generals, while wooden records and material remains offer glimpses of those who maintained the frontier. Together they show that conquest required repeated human work after the campaign ended. Control had to be supplied, recorded, cultivated, and defended season after season.

09Hexi became a Han frontier because a campaign was followed by institutions and people who kept them running. The victory of 121 BCE changed political control; it did not instantly create four stable towns or erase earlier inhabitants. The commanderies and guarded routes took shape over time, under the limits set by water, distance, and the need for food. That is the answer to how a corridor became a border system. A closer next question lies inside its daily work: what do surviving Han wooden slips reveal about the soldiers, couriers, and supplies that kept a distant post connected to the rest of the empire?

中文译文

01公元前121年，战争与归降改变了河西走廊的控制权。地图上的这句话看似简单，却引出更难的问题：军队的胜利怎样变成持续存在的边疆？上一篇考察了三角城：数百年前，人们已在那里筑起土墙。那道墙不能直接归入汉朝，也不能与某个后来的社群画上直线。它说明的是，走廊中早已有值得组织和守护的地方。到公元前二世纪，匈奴联盟控制着河西的大部分地区，其东、西地带由不同首领掌管。汉朝的扩张进入的是有人生活的土地，并非一条等待开通的空路。

02汉武帝的政府正在多条战线上与匈奴交战。河西之所以重要，是因为它位于汉朝腹地与更西方道路之间；同时，山地、河流和牧场也维系着原有居民。公元前121年，汉将霍去病率军在这一地区与匈奴作战。同年稍后，匈奴首领浑邪及其部众归降汉朝。战事记述主要出自汉朝史书；它们较多呈现朝廷的战略，却较少说明普通居民如何选择。这些记载并不能证明每个家庭都离开了，或所有地方人群的利益都相同。

03这一转变也给汉朝朝廷带来难题。遥远的土地不能只凭一份捷报维持控制。驻军需要粮食、水、住处，还需要跨越数百公里传送消息。绿洲能够支撑聚落，绿洲之间的干旱地带却使补给充满变数。于是，政府开始把军事控制转成行政控制。郡是一种区域行政单位，设有官员及下属县，可以登记户口、组织劳役和维持驻军。这是在一片早期聚落与游牧网络各循其方式运行的走廊上，叠加的新权力层级。

04后来的史料把河西四郡由东向西列为武威、张掖、酒泉、敦煌。它们并非在霍去病作战的那一天就成为一套完整制度。《汉书》记载先置酒泉郡，随后逐渐迁入居民，并分设其他郡。由于古代记载之间存在差异，现代学者对四郡中部分郡的确切设立年份，尤其是敦煌郡，仍有分歧。可以确定的是先后次序：先有军事取得，后有行政机构、移民和设防道路的逐步发展。地图上的郡名代表漫长的治理过程，而不是四次完全相同的命名行为。

05迁居使这套制度得以运转，也让人付出代价。汉朝官府把内地人口迁往新边疆，又派士兵驻守并耕种。对被送往西方的人而言，朝廷的决定意味着要在陌生而干旱的地方工作、居住。绿洲附近的农业需要可靠水源和持续劳作，绝非在田地四周划条线便能维持。对原有社群而言，新官员与军屯很可能改变土地、劳力和通行事务的决定方式。史料无法让我们重建每个当地家庭的反应，也不容许把匈奴部众、早期农民与新移民视为同一种人群。

06防守也从一场战斗变成日常事务。汉朝官府沿走廊修筑长墙、堡垒、烽燧和关口。在西端，玉门关与阳关逐渐承担起管理敦煌以西通行的职责。旅行者或使者会遇到检查关口；士兵则可能需要守望烽火或留下书面记录。汉代边塞的考古遗物与出土文书表明，这张网络既依靠兵器，也依靠日常行政。它让国家的通信和受保护的通行更加可靠，也使道路及其沿线居民受到更密切的管理。道路正在成为一套制度，而不只是穿越空旷土地的小径。

07新边疆并非只为贸易而建。汉朝统治者希望防范匈奴势力，并取得通向西域的路径；驻军和行政中心服务于这些目标。不过，同一套设施后来也能支持使者、商人、牲畜与货物沿绿洲移动。因此，如果过早给这段历史贴上熟悉的“丝绸之路”标签，就可能误导读者。正如前几篇所见，早在公元前121年之前，人与作物已穿行于河西部分地区。改变的是国家投入的规模，以及通过官署、农田、长墙和关口组成的持久体系引导通行的尝试。

08从三角城回望，两者的反差十分鲜明，却不能连成一条直接的家族史。古老土墙围起一处地方聚落；汉代边疆则把许多地点纳入远方权力之下。一些原属匈奴的人进入新的政治秩序，来自汉朝内地的新居民到来，较早的社群也很可能需要适应土地与通行规则的变化。幸存证据并不均衡：朝廷史书着重统治者与将领，木简和物质遗存则让维持边疆的人偶尔露面。两类资料共同表明，战事结束后，征服仍需要不断的人力付出。控制必须一季又一季地得到补给、记录、耕作和防守。

09河西之所以成为汉朝边疆，是因为战役之后出现了制度，以及维持制度运转的人。公元前121年的胜利改变了政治控制权，却没有立刻造出四座稳固城镇，也没有抹去原有居民。郡与受守护的道路是在水源、距离和粮食需求的限制下逐渐形成的。这回答了走廊怎样变成边境体系。下一篇可把目光投向其中的日常劳作：幸存的汉简能告诉我们什么，关于维持偏远驿站与帝国其他地区联系的士兵、传递者和物资？

阅读理解 · Reading check

  1. Why did the Han need more than a military victory to hold Hexi?
  2. Why does the article avoid giving one exact founding year for all four Hexi commanderies?
  3. How did the new frontier affect different groups of people?

参考答案 · Answers

  1. Troops and settlers needed food, water, shelter, communication, and officials. Commanderies, farms, forts, and passes developed to provide continuing control.
  2. The commanderies developed after the 121 BCE campaign, and ancient records differ about the dates of some of them, especially Dunhuang.
  3. Former Xiongnu subjects entered a changed political order, people from the Han interior were moved west, and existing communities faced new controls over land, work, and movement; individual responses are poorly documented.
