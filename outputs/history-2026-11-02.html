Five Months on the Road: What an Envoy's Pass Cannot Tell Us

五个月的路程：一份使团通行文书没说完的事

一份公元前18年的通行记录留下两次日期，也留下了旅程中大片未知。

English Story

01In 18 BCE, officials in Dunhuang issued a travel pass for envoys from Kangju and Shan. More than five months later, a record at Xuanquan Station placed the travelers on a westward journey. These two dates make an unusual pair. A pass could authorize movement, as yesterday's article showed, but it could not describe everything that happened after it was issued. What can a station record actually tell us about the time an embassy spent in Han territory? The answer requires attention to direction as well as dates, and to the large gap between two surviving pieces of writing.

02Xuanquan stood in Dunhuang Commandery, on the route between the western gates and the commanderies farther east. Its wooden slips were working documents, made to register people, supplies, and movements. They were not travel diaries. Historian Byung-Joon Kim compares the dates of several passes and station entries to ask how long western envoys remained within the Han realm. Jidong Yang likewise treats the Xuanquan manuscripts as evidence for the administration of foreign travelers. This kind of evidence is valuable because a dated entry can anchor a journey. It is limited because one station sees only the moment a party passes through it.

03In the case Kim discusses, the Dunhuang administrator issued the escort pass in January 18 BCE. Xuanquan registered the group in June of the same year. The entries are several months apart. That interval matters, but it cannot locate the travelers on every day between the records. The more important point is the direction written beside the later entry: west. A party heading from Dunhuang toward the Han capital would have traveled east through Xuanquan. The westward notation instead makes the June record evidence of a return journey, if the identification of the party is correct.

04That reading changes the question. The group did not necessarily spend five months moving slowly from Dunhuang to Xuanquan. It may have traveled east, completed an audience or other official business, and then crossed the station on its way home. Kim regards a visit to the court as the likely explanation. Yet the surviving entries do not give us a dated list of every stop, a complete account of whom the envoys met, or an inventory of what they exchanged. The gap is not an invitation to invent a banquet or bargain. It is the boundary between a plausible reconstruction and a documented event.

05The distinction matters because the earlier pass named an official purpose: envoys were to offer tribute to the emperor. That purpose says why officials could permit and escort them; it does not prove that every object they carried reached the capital. Other Xuanquan evidence, including the camel complaint discussed earlier in this series, shows that animals offered in the emperor's name could be valued at Jiuquan. A journey's formal destination and an object's practical destination were not always the same. But that other case cannot be pasted onto the envoys of 18 BCE. Their particular goods and any payment remain unrecorded in the two entries at issue here.

06For the people maintaining the road, a return trip still meant work. A station had to register the party again; an escort pass and its instructions could affect who received official service. Earlier articles followed the food accounts, horse records, and handovers that made such travel possible. The five-month interval reminds us that these tasks did not end when a delegation first crossed the frontier. Staff along the route could encounter the same visitors in both directions. We cannot name the workers who served this particular group, or calculate its full cost from these two documents, but the repeated crossing gives their routine labor historical weight.

07The case also warns against turning a surviving pass into a universal schedule. Some envoys entered with Han representatives; others arrived independently. Parties differed in size, rank, purpose, and access to provisions. A record from Kangju and Shan cannot supply an average length of stay for every visitor from the west. Kim's comparison gives a lower bound for this group's presence after the pass was issued, provided the June entry does record their return. It does not say where they spent each day. Historians can use such fragments to test a route and an interval, while leaving individual experiences and unrecorded exchanges open.

08A January pass and a June westward entry therefore tell a modest but meaningful story. Permission at Dunhuang was the beginning of an administered journey, not its entire history. A station record near the frontier can catch the same journey as it turns homeward, revealing time spent in a wider network of roads and offices. It cannot tell us exactly which goods changed hands. The next useful question lies in records that actually name an exchange or its accounting, rather than in filling the five silent months with assumed trade. For now, the return direction is the finding; the rest of the route remains a carefully bounded question.

中文译文

01公元前18年，敦煌官府为来自康居和山国的使者签发通行凭证。五个多月后，悬泉置的一条记录显示这些旅人正向西行。这两个日期构成一组不同寻常的线索。上一篇说明，凭证能够准许通行，却无法描述签发后发生的一切。驿站记录究竟能告诉我们使团在汉朝境内停留了多久？回答这个问题，既要看日期，也要看行进方向，还要正视两份留存文书之间的大片空白。

02悬泉置位于敦煌郡，在西部关口与更东边诸郡之间的道路上。这里的木简是登记人员、物资和行程的工作文书，不是旅行日记。历史学者金秉骏比较多份凭证和驿站记录的日期，追问西方使者在汉朝境内停留的时间。杨继东也把悬泉文书作为研究外国旅人如何受到管理的证据。这类材料的价值在于，有日期的记录能为行程提供锚点；局限在于，一座驿站只能看见队伍经过自己的那一刻。

03在金秉骏讨论的个案中，敦煌太守于公元前18年1月签发护送凭证；同年6月，悬泉置登记了这支队伍。两条记录相隔数月。这个时间间隔有意义，却不能告诉我们旅人其间每一天身在何处。更关键的是，后一条记录标明的方向是“西”。从敦煌前往汉朝京城的队伍经过悬泉置时应向东走。因此，如果两条记录确实指向同一队伍，六月的向西记录就成为返程的证据。

04这样解读后，问题便变了：这支队伍未必用了五个月才从敦煌缓缓走到悬泉置。他们可能先向东行，完成朝见或其他公务，再在归途中经过驿站。金秉骏认为朝廷之行是较可能的解释。但留存记录没有逐站注明日期，也没有完整列出使者见过谁、交换过什么。文书间的空白不能靠想象中的宴会或交易填上；它划出了合理推测与已获记录证实的事件之间的界线。

05这一区别很重要，因为较早的凭证写明了官方目的：使者要向皇帝进献。这个目的说明官府为何准许并护送他们，却不能证明他们携带的每件物品都抵达京城。本连载此前写过的骆驼申诉等悬泉材料表明，以皇帝名义进献的牲畜可能在酒泉估价。旅程的正式目的地与物品的实际去向，并不总是相同。但不能把那个个案硬套到公元前18年的使者身上：眼前两条记录并未写明他们具体带了什么、获得了什么。

06对维持道路运转的人来说，返程仍意味着工作。驿站必须再次登记队伍；护送凭证及其指令可能影响谁能获得官方服务。前几篇已追踪供食账目、马匹记录和文书交接，说明这些行程如何成为可能。五个月的间隔提醒我们，使团首次越过边境，并不意味着沿途工作就此结束。同一批来客可能在两个方向上与驿路人员相遇。仅凭这两份文书，我们无法说出服务这支队伍的人员姓名，也无法算出完整开支；但往返经过，使日常劳动的分量更清楚。

07这一案例也提醒我们，不能把一份留存凭证当作所有使团的统一时刻表。有些使者随汉朝代表入境，有些自行前来；队伍的规模、身份、目的和获得给养的资格各不相同。康居与山国这支队伍的记录，不能给所有西来旅人算出平均停留时间。如果六月的记录确属他们返程，金秉骏的比较至少说明：凭证签发后，他们在汉朝境内活动了相当长的一段时间；却无法交代每天身在何处。史家可以用碎片检验路线和时间跨度，同时保留个人经历与未记载交换的不确定性。

08因此，一月的凭证与六月向西的记录，讲述的是一个有限却有分量的故事：敦煌的许可只是受管理行程的起点，并非整段旅程的全部历史。靠近边境的驿站记录，在同一旅程转向归途时再次捕捉到它，使我们看见旅人曾在更广阔的道路和官署网络中活动一段时间。它仍不能说明究竟交换了哪些物品。下一步应寻找真正写明交换或核算的文书，而不是把五个月的沉默填成未经证实的贸易。眼下能够确认的是返程方向；其余路线仍是有边界的待解问题。
