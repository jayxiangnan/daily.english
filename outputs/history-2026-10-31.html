Who Ate at Xuanquan? Food and Rank on a Han Road

谁能在悬泉置用餐？汉代驿路上的食物与身份

一份鸡只出入簿，让我们追问汉代驿站的餐食从何而来、又供应给谁。

English Story

01In 62 BCE, someone at Xuanquan Station kept an account of chickens coming in and going out. The birds were not a decorative detail of life beside the road. They were part of the food available to people moving through a Han relay station near Dunhuang. Yesterday, a sick postal horse showed how staff maintained the means of travel. The chicken account raises the next question: once a messenger or envoy arrived, who could eat, and where did the meal come from? A station had to receive guests while also keeping enough supplies for the next group. Its kitchen therefore belonged to the same transport system as its stable.

02Xuanquan stood in Xiaogu county, within Dunhuang Commandery, on a route connecting the Hexi oases with lands farther west. Excavated wooden documents show a place that counted food as carefully as it counted horses. One study of a kitchen supervisor’s fragmentary account identifies more than 110 recipients, including messengers, military officials, and envoys. Most received one to three meals during a stay. The figure is drawn from surviving records, not a complete census of every visitor. Even so, it changes the image of an empty desert stop: feeding travelers required repeated decisions about people, portions, and stores.

03Law and practice did not speak in exactly the same voice. Han regulations on travel meals set limits on who was entitled to food at government stations. Official business mattered; a person traveling privately could not simply claim the same provision. The rules also limited excess attendants and treated misuse as a serious matter. The Xuanquan slips show how those broad rules met actual arrivals. A clerk needed to know a visitor’s standing, while kitchen workers needed ingredients ready before the next journey began. The documents do not reveal every conversation at the gate, but the distinction between eligible and ineligible guests was real.

04The station’s chicken register offers a narrower view of that work. A scholarly analysis dates its receipt and expenditure entries to 62 BCE and describes birds entering through local supply or purchase and leaving for meals. A chicken had to be obtained before it could be served; an entry for expenditure also had to be matched against what remained. This is an inference about the account’s administrative function, not evidence for a particular recipe or dining scene. Other foods appeared in station records too, but the chickens make the chain from acquisition to consumption unusually easy to follow.

05An exceptional visit tested the station on a different scale. A surviving account records food for Chang Hui, Marquis of Changluo, and his party before 61 BCE. It lists lambs, fish, chickens, beef, grain, and liquor. The study distinguishes items prepared from station stocks from items supplied by the county for this unusually large reception. This was not the ordinary meal of every courier. It shows that a prestigious traveler could draw resources from beyond the station itself, and that local administration helped carry the cost. The records count provisions; they do not tell us how every member of the party ate.

06These differences mattered to the communities around Xuanquan. Grain and animals did not appear at a station by themselves. County supply, purchases, storage, cooking, and recording connected local producers and workers to people passing through. The surviving papers make some officials and recipients visible, but usually leave the growers, carriers, and cooks unnamed. We can say that their labor was necessary to turn supplies into meals; we cannot assign a particular burden to an individual household from these records alone. A station’s welcome was therefore a managed use of resources, shaped by rank and official purpose, rather than unlimited hospitality.

07There is also a boundary to the evidence. The station accounts chiefly record official travelers and their attendants. A handbook of ancient economies notes that private traders are not shown as common customers in the surviving Xuanquan food accounts. That does not prove that merchants never passed nearby, or that no private lodging existed. It tells us what this official archive was built to record. The food slips are strongest when we ask how a state station provisioned authorized movement. They are weaker when we ask what every traveler on the wider road ate or where every caravan slept.

08The chicken account answers yesterday’s question with a system, not a menu. Xuanquan could feed travelers because supplies arrived, workers prepared them, and officials recorded who received them. A large party such as Chang Hui’s could draw on county support, while ordinary claims were restricted by rules. The result was a road open to certain movements at a cost borne and managed locally. The slips preserve neither a complete guest list nor the feelings of those who cooked and carried the food. They do show that Han travel depended on choices made at a kitchen door as surely as on the horses waiting outside.

中文译文

01公元前62年，悬泉置有人登记鸡只的收入与支出。这些鸡并非道路旁生活的点缀，而是敦煌附近这座汉代驿站接待行旅的食物来源之一。上一篇从一匹病传马写到人员如何维持交通工具；鸡只账簿则提出下一个问题：使者或官员抵达后，谁能用餐，饭食从哪里来？驿站既要接待眼前的来客，又要为下一批人保留供给。因此，厨房和马厩一样，都是交通体系的一部分。

02悬泉置在敦煌郡效谷县，处于连接河西绿洲与更西方地区的道路上。出土木简显示，这里核算食物如同核算马匹一样认真。一项对厨房主管残存账目的研究辨识出一百一十多名领食者，包括传信人、军中官员和使者；多数人在停留期间得到一至三餐。这个数字来自留存文书，并非全部来客的完整统计。即便如此，它也改变了我们对“荒漠中空荡驿站”的想象：给旅人供餐，需要反复确定人员、份额与库存。

03律令与实际记录的侧重点并不完全相同。汉代有关传食的规定限制了谁有资格在官办驿站领食：公务身份很重要，私人出行者不能直接索取同等给养；随员过多或滥用供给也受到约束。悬泉简让我们看到这些一般规则如何遇上具体来客。书吏需要辨明客人的身份，厨房人员则要在他们继续赶路前备好食材。文书没有保存门口的每段对话，但有资格与无资格之间的界线确实存在。

04鸡只出入簿让我们更近地看见这项工作。学术研究将其收支记录定在公元前62年，指出鸡来自地方供应或购买，随后被用于餐食。鸡必须先被取得，才能端上饭桌；支出也需要与剩余数量相核对。这是根据账簿用途作出的行政解释，并不能证明某一道菜或某一幕宴饮。驿站文书还记录其他食物，但鸡只尤其清楚地展现了从取得到消耗的链条。

05一次特别的到访让驿站面对另一种规模的接待。留存账目记录了公元前61年以前长罗侯常惠一行的食物，列有羊、鱼、鸡、牛肉、粮食与酒。研究区分了驿站库存中备出的物品，以及县里为这次规模异常的接待提供的物品。这并不是每名传信人都能享用的日常餐食。它表明，身份显赫的来客可以动用驿站之外的资源，地方行政也承担了供给成本。账目记录的是物资，不能告诉我们一行人每位成员究竟怎样用餐。

06这些差别也影响悬泉置周围的社群。粮食与牲畜不会自行来到驿站；县里的供应、购买、储存、烹饪和记账，把地方生产者与劳作者同过路的人联系起来。留存纸简使一些官员和领食者可见，却往往没有留下种植者、搬运者和厨役的名字。我们可以说，他们的劳动是把物资变成餐食的必要条件；却不能仅凭这些记录，把具体负担归到某一户人家。驿站的接待因此是按身份与公务目的管理的资源使用，而非无限供给。

07证据也有边界。驿站账目主要记录公务旅人及其随员。一部古代经济研究手册指出，留存的悬泉置食物账中，私人商人并非常见的领食者。这不能证明商人从未经过附近，也不能证明没有私人住宿设施；它说明这批官府档案原本要记录什么。若要了解官办驿站如何供应获准的出行，食物简牍很有力；若要知道整条道路上每个旅人吃什么、每支商队住哪里，它们就不足以回答。

08鸡只账簿对上一篇的问题给出的答案，是一套供给制度，而非一张菜单。悬泉置之所以能让旅人用餐，是因为物资到达、人员备餐、官吏记录领食者。常惠这样的大队伍可以得到县里的支持，普通领食要求则受律令限制。于是，某些人的远行得以持续，成本却需要在地方筹措与管理。简牍没有保存完整客人名册，也没有记下厨役与搬运者的感受；但它们确实说明，汉代交通既依赖门外待命的马，也依赖厨房门内的一次次安排。
