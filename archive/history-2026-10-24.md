# Before the Silk Roads: How the Hexi Corridor Took Shape｜丝路之前：河西走廊是怎样形成的？

河西走廊历史阅读 · 2026-10-24

山脉塑出通道，河水留下绿洲；人们如何在两者之间找到立足之地？

## English Story

**01** Look at a map of northwestern China and a question appears: why is there a long, usable passage beside some of the region's driest land? The Hexi Corridor, in today's Gansu, is often introduced as a part of the Silk Roads. But the routes came much later than the landscape. Before we can follow a traveler or an army, we need to understand the ground beneath them: mountains rising, low basins taking shape, and water reaching places where little rain falls. Geography did not draw a finished road. It created a set of possibilities, each with limits.

**02** To the south stand the Qilian Mountains, part of the active northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. Geological studies describe the Hexi area beside them as a complex foreland basin: as the mountains grew and faults moved, neighboring lowlands changed too. Sediment carried down from higher ground accumulated in parts of those basins. This was a long, uneven process, not an event that can be assigned one birthday. The corridor's western and eastern sections have different geological histories. Calling it a corridor is useful on a map; it should not make us imagine a single channel cut at one moment.

**03** The name also describes a contrast in relief. Southward, the Qilian ranges rise sharply; northward lie lower mountains, stony ground, and the margins of deserts. Between these harder stretches are basins and plains running broadly from east to west. They offer more room for movement than a high mountain crossing, but an open-looking plain is not necessarily hospitable. Wind, distance, and scarce surface water can turn a short line on a map into a difficult journey. The passage was therefore never a smooth strip of green linking one end to the other.

**04** Water changes the picture. The corridor is dry, with evaporation far exceeding local rainfall in much of the area. Yet the Qilian Mountains receive snow and rain at higher elevations. Runoff feeds three major inland river systems: the Shiyang in the east, the Heihe in the middle, and the Shule in the west. Their waters reach the lower ground and support oases. These are separate basins, not one river flowing along the entire corridor. The difference matters: a person could find a place to stop and cultivate, but could not assume that the next stretch would provide the same help.

**05** An oasis is more than a patch of color against sand. It is where water, soil, and human work meet. Its edges can shift as river flow, climate, and water use change; a river's arrival does not guarantee a settlement. People must decide where to live, what to grow, and how to share a supply that has to serve fields as well as households. These choices can sustain a community, while their costs are felt when water is scarce. The physical corridor offered openings; making a home or a dependable route through it required repeated effort.

**06** We can see that human story before the famous Han routes. At Sanjiao, a walled settlement in the corridor, researchers used radiocarbon dates and charred plant remains to study life on this dry ground. They date its construction to around 828 BC and identify barley, broomcorn millet, and foxtail millet among the crops consumed there. The findings do not tell us the names or private thoughts of its residents. They do show that people were building, growing food, and organizing their lives here centuries before the Han state established its later network of garrisons and roads.

**07** Later states changed the scale, not the underlying need for water. The UNESCO account of the Chang'an–Tianshan routes describes planned agricultural expansion in the Hexi area after the first century BC and canals carrying river water to settlements. Such works could support towns, garrisons, and travelers; they also depended on labor and control over a limited resource. The phrase ‘Silk Road’ may suggest a line drawn across empty space. The evidence points instead to a network built around inhabited places, competing needs, and the geography that made some connections possible and others costly.

**08** This distinction helps explain why the corridor mattered without turning it into a stage for one empire alone. Mountain growth and basin formation supplied the broad shape; rivers made some stopping places viable; communities and governments joined those places in different ways over time. None of these forces worked by itself. A mountain wall without water would not feed a settlement. A stream without the work of living people would not become a lasting route. The history of the corridor begins in that dependence, long before the best-known caravans crossed it.

**09** There is a quiet consequence in this origin story. The same water that opened a passage could be stretched thin when more people, fields, or journeys depended on it. The corridor connected regions because it offered places to live between difficult landscapes, not because the difficult landscapes vanished. Each generation inherited both an opportunity and a constraint. To understand what happened next, we can move from the shape of the land to a closer question: how did people protect and manage the water that allowed them to remain? The earliest settlements give us a place to begin.

## 中文译文

**01** 看中国西北地图，会生出一个问题：在这片极为干旱的土地旁，为何会有一条绵长而可以通行的地带？今天甘肃境内的河西走廊，常被作为丝绸之路的一段来介绍。但道路出现得远比这片地形晚。在追随旅人或军队之前，我们需要先看他们脚下的土地：山脉如何隆起，低地如何成形，水又如何抵达少雨之处。地理没有画出一条现成的道路；它给出了若干可能，每一种都有自己的限制。

**02** 走廊南侧是祁连山，它位于青藏高原东北缘持续活动的地带。地质研究把相邻的河西地区描述为结构复杂的前陆盆地：山脉生长、断层活动时，旁边的低地也随之改变。从高处搬运下来的沉积物，又在一些盆地里不断堆积。这是漫长而不均匀的过程，无法给整条走廊指定一个“生日”。走廊的东西两段也有不同的地质历史。在地图上称它为“走廊”很方便，却不该因此想象出一条在同一时刻凿成的槽道。

**03** “走廊”一名也写出了地势上的反差：向南，祁连山陡然升高；向北，则是较低的山地、砾石地和沙漠边缘。其间的盆地和平原，大体沿东西方向展开。与翻越高山相比，它们为移动提供了更大的空间；但看起来开阔的平原，并不一定宜人。风、路程和稀少的地表水，能让地图上的一小段距离变成艰难的行程。因此，这片通道从来不是一条首尾相接的绿色坦途。

**04** 水改变了这幅图景。走廊气候干旱，许多地方的蒸发量远高于当地降水量；但海拔较高的祁连山会迎来雪和雨。径流汇入三大内陆河水系：东部的石羊河、中部的黑河和西部的疏勒河。河水到达较低的地带，支撑起绿洲。它们属于彼此分开的流域，并非一条贯穿整条走廊的河。这一区别很要紧：人们可以找到停留和耕作之处，却不能认定下一段路也会得到同样的帮助。

**05** 绿洲不只是沙地上的一块绿色。它是水、土壤和人的劳作相遇的地方。河流流量、气候和用水方式一变，绿洲边界也可能移动；有河水到来，并不保证聚落就能形成。人们必须决定住在哪里、种什么，以及如何在农田与家庭之间分配水。这样的选择可以维系社群；水紧缺时，代价也会落到社群身上。自然地形提供了立足之处，但要在其中安家，或维持可靠的通行路线，还需要持续付出。

**06** 在著名的汉代道路出现之前，我们已能看到人的故事。研究人员在走廊内的三角城遗址，利用放射性碳测年和炭化植物遗存，考察人们怎样在这片干旱土地上生活。他们把城址的建造年代定在约公元前828年，并在食用作物中辨认出大麦、黍和粟。这些发现没有告诉我们居民的姓名或内心想法，却表明：早在汉朝建立后来的屯戍与道路网络之前数百年，人们就在这里修筑、种植，并安排自己的生活。

**07** 后来的国家改变了开发规模，却没有改变对水的根本依赖。联合国教科文组织关于长安—天山廊道路网的资料提到，公元前一世纪之后，河西地区有规划的农业扩展，也有把河水引向聚落的沟渠。这些工程能够支撑城镇、戍所和旅人，同时也依赖劳力与对有限水源的调配。“丝绸之路”这个名称，容易让人想到一条画过空白地带的线。史料呈现的却是另一幅图景：一张围绕有人居住的地方、不同的需求，以及使某些联系成为可能又使另一些联系代价高昂的地理条件，逐步建立的路网。

**08** 分清这些层次，我们就能理解走廊为何重要，而不必把它写成某一个帝国独有的舞台。山脉隆起与盆地形成，给出大体形状；河流使一些停留之处能够维持生活；不同时期的社群与政权，又以不同方式连接这些地方。没有哪一种力量能单独完成这一切。只有山而没有水，便难以养活聚落；只有溪流而没有人的劳作，也不会自然形成持久的路线。走廊的历史，正是从这种相互依存开始的，远早于最为人熟知的商队穿行其间。

**09** 这段起源故事还有一个不喧哗的后果：正是让通道得以出现的水，在更多人、农田与旅程依赖它时，也可能变得不够用。走廊能够连接不同地区，是因为艰难的地形之间存在可供生活的地方，而不是因为艰难本身消失了。每一代人都继承了机会，也继承了限制。接下来，我们可以从土地的形状走向一个更贴近生活的问题：人们如何保护、调配使自己能够留下的水？早期聚落提供了观察的起点。

## 重点表达 · Key expressions

- **take shape**：逐渐成形；强调形成是过程，而非一次完成
- **a foreland basin**：前陆盆地；山脉前缘受构造作用影响的低地区域
- **far exceed local rainfall**：远高于当地降水量；用于比较蒸发与降水
- **a dependable route**：可长期依靠的通行路线
- **be stretched thin**：因需求增加而捉襟见肘（这里指有限水源）

## 阅读理解 · Reading check

1. Why does the article say the corridor cannot be given one geological “birthday”?
2. What is the difference between the three inland river systems and a single river road?
3. What does the Sanjiao evidence show, and what can it not tell us?

### 参考答案 · Answers

1. Its basins and mountains took shape through long, uneven geological processes, and different sections have different histories.
2. The Shiyang, Heihe, and Shule belong to separate basins; oases provide stopping places, but water is not continuous along the whole corridor.
3. It shows that people built a walled settlement and consumed several crops before the Han routes. It cannot reveal residents’ names or private thoughts.
